Why these are free to use

  1. The artworks are in the public domain. Copyright in a painting lasts, at most, for the artist's life plus 70 years. The most recent artists here — Repin (d. 1930) and Renoir (d. 1919) — passed that threshold decades ago, so every painting is in the public domain worldwide.
  2. Faithful photographs of flat artworks add no new copyright. A straight, faithful photographic reproduction of a 2-D public-domain work does not create a fresh copyright in the United States (Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel, 1999) or in the EU (Directive 2019/790, Article 14). So the high-resolution scans are free to use as well.
  3. Sources are chosen to avoid even contractual friction. Where a museum runs a formal open-access programme the image is taken from there and dedicated to the public domain under CC0 (Art Institute of Chicago, The Met) or released as public-domain open data (National Gallery of Art, Rijksmuseum). The rest come from Wikimedia Commons, where the public-domain status is documented on each file page. This sidesteps the “terms of use” fine print that some museum websites attach to their own downloads — a contractual matter, separate from copyright.

Caveats

  • This is general information, not legal advice. For commercial App Store distribution, a lawyer's sign-off is the only way to be 100% certain.
  • A museum's website terms of use can restrict downloads from that site even when the underlying artwork is public domain. Using the CC0 / open-access sources or Wikimedia Commons above keeps the project clear of that.
  • Dünya Üçer's drawings are not public domain — they are the family's, used by permission.

The icon is an eye inside a magnifying glass, and the eye is a motif from Anatolian kilims, the one that looks back. The game asks for the same thing: look at one painting for longer than you meant to.
