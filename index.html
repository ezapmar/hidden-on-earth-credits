Three rules the game is built on.

Nothing is drawn on top

Every thing to find is a real detail of the artwork. The monkey on Seurat's riverbank has been there since 1884.

Every count is exact

If the game says three dogs, the painting has three dogs. A thing we cannot count for certain is left out.

No clock, nothing to lose

No timer, no score, no lives. A wrong tap makes a soft ripple. The hint pulses on one hiding place; the child still has to see it.

It started with a tablet we could not agree on.

My son Dünya is five and a half. He has loved seek-and-find books since he was two, and we have been through every one we could find, at every age they are printed for. Then a tablet came into the house, and his mother and I spent weeks disagreeing about what he should be allowed to play on it. The answer was already on his shelf: why not seek-and-find?

That is why there is no clock, why the taps are forgiving and why every word is said aloud. Dünya chooses the paintings and decides where the game goes next, so nobody else knows what is coming. Four of his own drawings are in it beside Van Gogh and Hokusai, credited to Dünya Üçer, 2026.

The name is his as well. Dünya is the Turkish word for the earth, so the game is Dünya'da Saklı: hidden on earth, and hidden in Dünya.

The icon is an eye inside a magnifying glass, and the eye is the Anatolian kilim motif that looks back. The game asks for the same thing: look at one painting for longer than you meant to.
